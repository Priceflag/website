How we measure

Every Priceflag forecast is a range, fitted to your own orders and checked against real orders as the change rolls out. This page explains each part, and where the numbers on our homepage come from.

What a forecast is

Before a price change goes live, Priceflag shows a range for the change in orders and in profit over the 90 days after the change. It is always a range, never a single number, because the width of the range is what you have to plan around.

Each forecast also gives you a breakeven in plain words: you can lose up to a certain share of orders and still make the same profit.

Example: a product that costs $9.60 to make goes from $24.00 to $27.00. The profit per order rises from $14.40 to $17.40, so you can lose up to 17.2% of orders and still make the same profit.

The 90-day window and the baseline

A forecast covers the 90 days after the change. It compares against the old price over the same 90 days: what the product would have sold had you left its price alone.

Where the forecast comes from

Priceflag fits each forecast to your store's own order history, up to the last 180 days. Products that sell more give tighter ranges.

If a product has too few orders for a trustworthy forecast, Priceflag says so and takes smaller steps instead of showing confidence it does not have.

How a rollout is checked

A change rolls out by product and over time, never by visitor. Every shopper sees the same price.

  1. 25%of the products you picked
  2. 50%of the products you picked
  3. Allof the products you picked

At each stage, real daily orders are compared against the range the forecast expected. Two bad days in a row trip the guardrail. In the beta, a tripped guardrail pauses the rollout and emails you. It never rolls a price back by itself; you decide what happens next.

What “simulated” means

The numbers on our homepage come from simulated test rollouts, not from live customer stores. Here is what each one means, in simulation.

93% of forecast ranges contained the outcome
Of the forecast ranges in the simulation, 93% contained the simulated outcome.
4.7-point median miss
The typical distance, in percentage points, between the forecast and the simulated outcome.
82% of demand shocks caught, median day 6
The share of simulated demand shocks the guardrail caught, and the median day of the rollout on which it caught them.
7% false pauses
A false pause is a pause triggered when the price was in fact fine.
100% of prices restored after rollback
Every simulated rollback put the old price back.
